# Sources used in the v2 deck (verified)

Every source was opened and checked by a second reviewer (title, authors, year, journal, DOI, and that the paper supports the sentence). None of them tested exactly our setup: they justify trying an idea, and our own runs decide whether it works here.

## Passive torque: why we measure it inside the trial

- Duong B, Low M, Moseley AM, Lee RY, Herbert RD. Time course of stress relaxation and recovery in human ankles. Clinical Biomechanics 2001;16(7):601-607. PMID 11470302. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0268-0033(01)00043-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0268-0033(01)00043-2)
  - What it shows: When a relaxed ankle was held at a fixed stretched angle for 20 minutes, the passive torque kept falling toward about 58% of its starting value (half of that drop took about 5 minutes), and it partly came back within 2 minutes once the stretch was released.
- McNair PJ, Dombroski EW, Hewson DJ, Stanley SN. Stretching at the ankle joint: viscoelastic responses to holds and continuous passive motion. Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise 2001;33(3):354-358. PMID 11252058. [https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200103000-00003](https://doi.org/10.1097/00005768-200103000-00003)
  - What it shows: When the calf muscles were held stretched for a total of 60 seconds, the passive resisting force dropped by about 20%, and most of that drop happened in the first 20 seconds of each hold.
- Tian M, Hoang PD, Gandevia SC, Bilston LE, Herbert RD. Stress relaxation of human ankles is only minimally affected by knee and ankle angle. Journal of Biomechanics 2010;43(5):990-993. PMID 20003980. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.11.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.11.017)
  - What it shows: Relaxed human ankles showed passive torque relaxing over time at every knee and ankle angle tested, and the relative amount of relaxation was roughly the same whatever the angle.
- Magnusson SP, Simonsen EB, Dyhre-Poulsen P, Aagaard P, Mohr T, Kjaer M. Viscoelastic stress relaxation during static stretch in human skeletal muscle in the absence of EMG activity. Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports 1996;6(6):323-328. PMID 9046541. [https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.1996.tb00101.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.1996.tb00101.x)
  - What it shows: During a 90-second stretch held at a fixed angle, passive torque fell by about a third even with no muscle activity, showing the drop comes from the tissue itself rather than the nervous system.
- Kay AD, Blazevich AJ. Isometric contractions reduce plantar flexor moment, Achilles tendon stiffness, and neuromuscular activity but remove the subsequent effects of stretch. Journal of Applied Physiology 2009;107(4):1181-1189. PMID 19644033. [https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00281.2009](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00281.2009)
  - What it shows: After six 8-second maximal calf contractions, the passive ankle torque measured on a dynamometer was about 5% lower, and Achilles tendon stiffness about 11% lower, than before.
- Hagbarth KE, Hagglund JV, Nordin M, Wallin EU. Thixotropic behaviour of human finger flexor muscles with accompanying changes in spindle and reflex responses to stretch. Journal of Physiology 1985;368:323-342. PMID 2934547. [https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1985.sp015860](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1985.sp015860)
  - What it shows: The resting stiffness of relaxed finger muscles depended on what they had just done: voluntary isometric contractions made them looser, and these after-effects faded over several minutes.
- Lakie M, Campbell KS. Muscle thixotropy - where are we now? Journal of Applied Physiology 2019;126(6):1790-1799. PMID 31070959; PMCID PMC6734056. [https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00788.2018](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00788.2018)
  - What it shows: Relaxed muscle gets stiffer the longer it stays still and loosens again when it is moved, actively or passively, so its resting resistance depends on its recent history.
- Proske U, Morgan DL, Gregory JE. Thixotropy in skeletal muscle and in muscle spindles: a review. Progress in Neurobiology 1993;41(6):705-721. PMID 8140258. [https://doi.org/10.1016/0301-0082(93)90032-N](https://doi.org/10.1016/0301-0082(93)90032-N) **(not verified: not used on the slides)**
  - What it shows: This classic review explains that a relaxed muscle's stiffness and resting tension depend on its previous contractions and length changes, a property called thixotropy.
- Ryan ED, Beck TW, Herda TJ, Hull HR, Hartman MJ, Costa PB, Defreitas JM, Stout JR, Cramer JT. The time course of musculotendinous stiffness responses following different durations of passive stretching. Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy 2008;38(10):632-639. PMID 18827325. [https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2008.2843](https://doi.org/10.2519/jospt.2008.2843)
  - What it shows: After the calf muscles were passively stretched on a Biodex dynamometer, their stiffness dropped right away and was back to normal within 10 to 20 minutes, depending on how long the stretch lasted.
- Lakie M, Robson LG. Thixotropic changes in human muscle stiffness and the effects of fatigue. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Physiology 1988;73(4):487-500. PMID 3174910. [https://doi.org/10.1113/expphysiol.1988.sp003169](https://doi.org/10.1113/expphysiol.1988.sp003169)
  - What it shows: Relaxed finger muscles kept stiffening for at least 30 minutes of rest, and movement undid this right away, although in this study isometric efforts did not.
- Walker J, Bissas A, Wainwright B, Hanley B, Cronin NJ. Repeatability and sensitivity of passive mechanical stiffness measurements in the triceps surae muscle-tendon complex. Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports 2022;32(1):83-93. PMID 34606650. [https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14070](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14070)
  - What it shows: When the same people had their passive calf stiffness measured on a dynamometer on separate days, the results agreed only moderately, so the authors warn against using it as a stable reference across sessions.
- Hoang PD, Gorman RB, Todd G, Gandevia SC, Herbert RD. A new method for measuring passive length-tension properties of human gastrocnemius muscle in vivo. Journal of Biomechanics 2005;38(6):1333-1341. PMID 15863118. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.05.046](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2004.05.046)
  - What it shows: Passive calf-muscle tension curves worked out from ankle torque measurements were more repeatable on the same day than a week later (error about 3% versus 6% of maximal passive tension).
- Nordez A, Foure A, Dombroski EW, Mariot JP, Cornu C, McNair PJ. Improvements to Hoang et al.'s method for measuring passive length-tension properties of human gastrocnemius muscle in vivo. Journal of Biomechanics 2010;43(2):379-382. PMID 19782365. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.07.034](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.07.034)
  - What it shows: When passive calf-muscle stiffness was measured on two different days, the values typically differed by about 6% (ICC about 0.8), which is decent agreement but not identical.
- Anderson DE, Nussbaum MA, Madigan ML. A new method for gravity correction of dynamometer data and determining passive elastic moments at the joint. Journal of Biomechanics 2010;43(6):1220-1223. PMID 20047749. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.11.036](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.11.036)
  - What it shows: The torque a dynamometer reads on a relaxed limb is the weight of the limb and attachment plus the passive stretch of the tissues, so the two have to be separated before you can call it passive tissue torque.
- Winter DA, Wells RP, Orr GW. Errors in the use of isokinetic dynamometers. European Journal of Applied Physiology and Occupational Physiology 1981;46(4):397-408. PMID 7196329. [https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00422127](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00422127)
  - What it shows: If you do not correct for the weight of the limb, the dynamometer gives wrong torque values, and the error is biggest when the person is producing little or no force.
- Herzog W. The relation between the resultant moments at a joint and the moments measured by an isokinetic dynamometer. Journal of Biomechanics 1988;21(1):5-12. PMID 3339027. [https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(88)90185-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9290(88)90185-6)
  - What it shows: The torque shown by a dynamometer is not the same as the torque at the joint, mainly because of gravity, inertia and give in the attachment, so it must be corrected before drawing conclusions about the muscles.

## EMG size and auto-gain

- De Luca CJ. The use of surface electromyography in biomechanics. J Appl Biomech. 1997;13(2):135-163. [https://doi.org/10.1123/jab.13.2.135](https://doi.org/10.1123/jab.13.2.135)
  - What it shows: A classic review that lists the many things besides muscle effort that change the EMG signal, such as where the electrode sits, sweat on the skin and the fat layer under it.
- Farina D, Merletti R, Enoka RM. The extraction of neural strategies from the surface EMG. J Appl Physiol. 2004;96(4):1486-1495. [https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01070.2003](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01070.2003)
  - What it shows: Many things besides the brain's command to the muscle affect surface EMG, so EMG size is easy to misread as 'how hard the muscle is working'.
- Campanini I, Merlo A, Degola P, Merletti R, Vezzosi G, Farina D. Effect of electrode location on EMG signal envelope in leg muscles during gait. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2007;17(4):515-526. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2006.06.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2006.06.001)
  - What it shows: Moving the electrodes to neighbouring spots 2 cm apart on the calf muscles changed the peak EMG by about 15-25% on average during the same walking task.
- Hewson DJ, Hogrel JY, Langeron Y, Duchene J. Evolution in impedance at the electrode-skin interface of two types of surface EMG electrodes during long-term recordings. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2003;13(3):273-279. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S1050-6411(02)00097-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1050-6411(02)00097-4)
  - What it shows: With electrodes left on the shin for 2 hours, the electrode-skin contact steadily changed for one of the two electrode types tested, and by very different amounts from person to person.
- Winkel J, Jorgensen K. Significance of skin temperature changes in surface electromyography. Eur J Appl Physiol Occup Physiol. 1991;63(5):345-348. [https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00364460](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00364460)
  - What it shows: When the skin over the soleus (a calf muscle) was cooler, the EMG amplitude roughly doubled for the same exercise.
- Abdoli-Eramaki M, Damecour C, Christenson J, Stevenson J. The effect of perspiration on the sEMG amplitude and power spectrum. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2012;22(6):908-913. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2012.04.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2012.04.009)
  - What it shows: As more (artificial) sweat was added under the electrodes, the EMG from a maximum-effort contraction got steadily smaller.
- Kuiken TA, Lowery MM, Stoykov NS. The effect of subcutaneous fat on myoelectric signal amplitude and cross-talk. Prosthet Orthot Int. 2003;27(1):48-54. [https://doi.org/10.3109/03093640309167976](https://doi.org/10.3109/03093640309167976)
  - What it shows: In a computer model of the arm, the thicker the fat layer between muscle and electrode, the smaller the EMG signal: 3 mm of fat cut it by about a third.
- Moritani T, Muro M, Nagata A. Intramuscular and surface electromyogram changes during muscle fatigue. J Appl Physiol. 1986;60(4):1179-1185. [https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1986.60.4.1179](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1986.60.4.1179)
  - What it shows: When people held the same half-maximum force for a long time and got tired, their EMG amplitude went up even though the force stayed the same.
- Dimitrova NA, Dimitrov GV. Interpretation of EMG changes with fatigue: facts, pitfalls, and fallacies. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2003;13(1):13-36. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S1050-6411(02)00083-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1050-6411(02)00083-4)
  - What it shows: With fatigue, studies have found EMG amplitude going up, staying about the same, or going down, so fatigue makes EMG size an unreliable measure of force.
- Enoka RM, Duchateau J. Muscle fatigue: what, why and how it influences muscle function. J Physiol. 2008;586(1):11-23. [https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.139477](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.2007.139477)
  - What it shows: Muscle fatigue has many different causes, and which ones matter depends on the task being done.
- Vigotsky AD, Halperin I, Lehman GJ, Trajano GS, Vieira TM. Interpreting signal amplitudes in surface electromyography studies in sport and rehabilitation sciences. Front Physiol. 2018;8:985. [https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2017.00985)
  - What it shows: An easy-to-read review explaining that comparing raw EMG sizes is only fair if electrode position, skin/fat, crosstalk and normalisation are all controlled.
- Tkach D, Huang H, Kuiken TA. Study of stability of time-domain features for electromyographic pattern recognition. J NeuroEng Rehabil. 2010;7:21. [https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-7-21](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-7-21)
  - What it shows: Shifting the electrodes or changing effort changed the EMG features enough to noticeably lower the accuracy of an EMG-based model.
- Schulte RV, Zondag M, Buurke JH, Prinsen EC. Multi-day EMG-based knee joint torque estimation using hybrid neuromusculoskeletal modelling and convolutional neural networks. Front Robot AI. 2022;9:869476. [https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2022.869476](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2022.869476)
  - What it shows: A neural network trained on one day to predict knee torque from EMG had about 6% error on that day but about 10-11% on later days, even though the EMG was rescaled to a max-effort test every day.
- Schulte RV, Prinsen EC, Buurke JH, Poel M. Adaptive lower limb pattern recognition for multi-day control. Sensors (Basel). 2022;22(17):6351. [https://doi.org/10.3390/s22176351](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22176351)
  - What it shows: A leg EMG system made significantly more errors on the second day than the first (a drift the authors put down to things like fatigue, re-applied sensors and changing skin), and adapting the model reduced this.
- Kaufmann P, Englehart K, Platzner M. Fluctuating EMG signals: investigating long-term effects of pattern matching algorithms. Conf Proc IEEE Eng Med Biol Soc. 2010;2010:6357-6360. [https://doi.org/10.1109/IEMBS.2010.5627288](https://doi.org/10.1109/IEMBS.2010.5627288)
  - What it shows: Over 21 days of recordings, models trained at the start tended to get worse on later days, which points to the EMG itself changing over time.
- Waris A, Niazi IK, Jamil M, Englehart K, Jensen W, Kamavuako EN. Multiday evaluation of techniques for EMG-based classification of hand motions. IEEE J Biomed Health Inform. 2019;23(4):1526-1534. [https://doi.org/10.1109/JBHI.2018.2864335](https://doi.org/10.1109/JBHI.2018.2864335)
  - What it shows: Over 7 days, the best model (a neural network) made more than twice as many errors when tested on a different day than on the day it was trained (about 22% vs 9% with surface EMG).
- Burden A. How should we normalize electromyograms obtained from healthy participants? What we have learned from over 25 years of research. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2010;20(6):1023-1035. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2010.07.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2010.07.004)
  - What it shows: Raw EMG has to be rescaled (normalised) before you can compare recordings when the electrodes are put back on, or between muscles or people.
- Besomi M, Hodges PW, Clancy EA, Van Dieen J, Hug F, Lowery M, Merletti R, Sogaard K, Wrigley T, Besier T, et al. Consensus for experimental design in electromyography (CEDE) project: Amplitude normalization matrix. J Electromyogr Kinesiol. 2020;53:102438. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2020.102438](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2020.102438)
  - What it shows: An international expert panel says EMG amplitude needs normalising to compare people, muscles, sessions or electrode positions, and lists six ways to do it.
- Lehman GJ, McGill SM. The importance of normalization in the interpretation of surface electromyography: a proof of principle. J Manipulative Physiol Ther. 1999;22(7):444-446. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0161-4754(99)70032-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0161-4754(99)70032-1)
  - What it shows: A simple example where raw EMG suggested a big difference between two parts of a stomach muscle, and the difference disappeared once the signals were normalised.
- Yang JF, Winter DA. Electromyographic amplitude normalization methods: improving their sensitivity as diagnostic tools in gait analysis. Arch Phys Med Rehabil. 1984;65(9):517-521. [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6477083/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6477083/)
  - What it shows: Scaling each person's walking EMG (including the soleus) by its own peak or average, instead of a half-max test contraction, greatly reduced the differences between people.
- Koller JR, Jacobs DA, Ferris DP, Remy CD. Learning to walk with an adaptive gain proportional myoelectric controller for a robotic ankle exoskeleton. J NeuroEng Rehabil. 2015;12:97. [https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-015-0086-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-015-0086-5)
  - What it shows: An ankle exoskeleton driven by calf (soleus) EMG re-set its EMG scaling every step, based on the peaks of the last 50 steps, so that the user's typical peak EMG always gave the exoskeleton's full push.
- Tanaka T, Nambu I, Maruyama Y, Wada Y. Sliding-window normalization to improve the performance of machine-learning models for real-time motion prediction using electromyography. Sensors (Basel). 2022;22(13):5005. [https://doi.org/10.3390/s22135005](https://doi.org/10.3390/s22135005)
  - What it shows: Rescaling EMG using only the most recent window of data (no calibration needed) made a machine-learning model as accurate as normal rescaling and far more accurate than no rescaling (77.7% vs 56.2%).
- Tanaka T, Nambu I, Wada Y. Mitigating the impact of electrode shift on classification performance in electromyography applications using sliding-window normalization. Sensors (Basel). 2025;25(13):4119. [https://doi.org/10.3390/s25134119](https://doi.org/10.3390/s25134119)
  - What it shows: Running normalisation almost cancelled the accuracy loss caused by moving the electrodes, without retraining the model.
- Du Y, Jin W, Wei W, Hu Y, Geng W. Surface EMG-based inter-session gesture recognition enhanced by deep domain adaptation. Sensors (Basel). 2017;17(3):458. [https://doi.org/10.3390/s17030458](https://doi.org/10.3390/s17030458)
  - What it shows: A deep network did much better on a new recording session (about 82% vs 63% accuracy) just by recalculating its internal scaling (mean and spread) from the new session's unlabelled EMG.
- Moore BC, Glasberg BR, Stone MA. Optimization of a slow-acting automatic gain control system for use in hearing aids. Br J Audiol. 1991;25(3):171-182. [https://doi.org/10.3109/03005369109079851](https://doi.org/10.3109/03005369109079851)
  - What it shows: Hearing aids use a slow automatic gain control that keeps turning the volume up or down so speech comes out at a steady level, however loud the input is.
- Wheeler HA. Automatic volume control for radio receiving sets. Proc IRE. 1928;16(1):30-34. [https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1928.221286](https://doi.org/10.1109/JRPROC.1928.221286)
  - What it shows: The original radio paper on automatically adjusting a receiver's amplification so the sound stays at a steady volume whether the incoming signal is strong or weak (or fading).
- Jonsson B. Measurement and evaluation of local muscular strain in the shoulder during constrained work. J Hum Ergol (Tokyo). 1982;11(1):73-88. [https://doi.org/10.11183/jhe1972.11.73](https://doi.org/10.11183/jhe1972.11.73)
  - What it shows: Workplace EMG studies summarise muscle load using levels from the EMG amplitude distribution (10th, 50th and 90th percentile), using the 90th percentile as the 'peak' because the very top value was often unreliable.

## Model, fitting and scoring

- Hochreiter S, Schmidhuber J. Long short-term memory. Neural Computation 9(8):1735-1780. [https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1997.9.8.1735)
  - What it shows: This is the paper that introduced the LSTM, a type of neural network with a built-in memory and 'gates' that lets it learn patterns spread over long stretches of time.
- Gers FA, Schmidhuber J, Cummins F. Learning to forget: continual prediction with LSTM. Neural Computation 12(10):2451-2471. [https://doi.org/10.1162/089976600300015015](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976600300015015)
  - What it shows: This paper added the 'forget gate', which lets an LSTM learn when to clear out old memory it no longer needs, and standard LSTMs today include it.
- Greff K, Srivastava RK, Koutnik J, Steunebrink BR, Schmidhuber J. LSTM: A search space odyssey. IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems 28(10):2222-2232. [https://doi.org/10.1109/TNNLS.2016.2582924](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNNLS.2016.2582924)
  - What it shows: In a large test of 5400 training runs, none of eight LSTM variants did clearly better than the standard LSTM, and the forget gate was one of its most important parts.
- Jacobs RA, Jordan MI, Nowlan SJ, Hinton GE. Adaptive mixtures of local experts. Neural Computation 3(1):79-87. [https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1991.3.1.79](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1991.3.1.79)
  - What it shows: This is the classic paper usually cited for the 'mixture of experts', where several small expert networks each learn part of the problem and a separate gating network decides, for each input, how much each expert counts or which one to use.
- Jordan MI, Jacobs RA. Hierarchical mixtures of experts and the EM algorithm. Neural Computation 6(2):181-214. [https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1994.6.2.181](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco.1994.6.2.181)
  - What it shows: This paper arranges the experts in a tree, where the mixing weights (the gate) are themselves simple models of the input, and tests it on robot-arm dynamics.
- Yuksel SE, Wilson JN, Gader PD. Twenty years of mixture of experts. IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems 23(8):1177-1193. [https://doi.org/10.1109/TNNLS.2012.2200299](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNNLS.2012.2200299)
  - What it shows: A broad review of 20 years of mixture-of-experts research for both prediction (regression) and classification, showing the idea is well established and widely used.
- Kendall A, Gal Y, Cipolla R. Multi-task learning using uncertainty to weigh losses for scene geometry and semantics. Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), pp. 7482-7491. [https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2018.00781](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2018.00781)
  - What it shows: When one network learns several tasks at once, each task's squared error is multiplied by 1/(2 sigma^2), where sigma is how noisy that task is, so noisier tasks count for less and cannot take over training.
- Cochran WG. The combination of estimates from different experiments. Biometrics 10(1):101-129. [https://doi.org/10.2307/3001666](https://doi.org/10.2307/3001666)
  - What it shows: A classic statistics paper on how to combine results from several experiments that differ in precision using a weighted average instead of a plain average.
- Aitken AC. On least squares and linear combination of observations. Proceedings of the Royal Society of Edinburgh 55:42-48. [https://doi.org/10.1017/S0370164600014346](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0370164600014346) **(not verified: not used on the slides)**
  - What it shows: This is usually cited as the origin of generalized least squares, which weights each data point by how uncertain it is instead of treating all points equally.
- Bland JM, Altman DG. Statistical methods for assessing agreement between two methods of clinical measurement. The Lancet 327(8476):307-310. [https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(86)90837-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(86)90837-8)
  - What it shows: The correlation between two measurements depends on how wide a range of values is in the sample: a wide range gives a higher correlation than a narrow one, even when the agreement is just as good.
- Schaefli B, Gupta HV. Do Nash values have value? Hydrological Processes 21(15):2075-2080. [https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.6825](https://doi.org/10.1002/hyp.6825)
  - What it shows: The Nash-Sutcliffe score (the same formula as R² for predictions) comes out high on signals with big swings even for a crude model, while a signal that stays near a constant level only scores well if its small wiggles are predicted.
- Gupta HV, Kling H, Yilmaz KK, Martinez GF. Decomposition of the mean squared error and NSE performance criteria: implications for improving hydrological modelling. Journal of Hydrology 377(1-2):80-91. [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2009.08.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhydrol.2009.08.003)
  - What it shows: This R²-style score is 1 minus (mean squared error divided by the variance of the measurements), so the same error scores better when the measurements swing more, and the authors warn against comparing these scores between datasets whose signals swing by different amounts.
- Kvalseth TO. Cautionary note about R². The American Statistician 39(4):279-285. [https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1985.10479448](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1985.10479448)
  - What it shows: R² is the most widely used goodness-of-fit number but is often misused, partly because the different R² formulas only agree for linear models with an intercept.
- Clancy EA, Liu L, Liu P, Moyer DVZ. Identification of constant-posture EMG-torque relationship about the elbow using nonlinear dynamic models. IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering 59(1):205-212. [https://doi.org/10.1109/TBME.2011.2170423](https://doi.org/10.1109/TBME.2011.2170423)
  - What it shows: Using biceps and triceps EMG from 33 people with the elbow held still, the best models estimated elbow torque to within about 4.7% of maximum voluntary contraction.
- Ziai A, Menon C. Comparison of regression models for estimation of isometric wrist joint torques using surface electromyography. Journal of NeuroEngineering and Rehabilitation 8:56. [https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-8-56](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-0003-8-56)
