A · Setup

What we predict

The dynamometer measures the total torque at the ankle. Part of it is passive: stretched tissues pull even when the person is relaxed, and this part depends on the ankle position. The rest is voluntary: what the muscles produce when the person pushes.

Definitions we use everywhere

  • Voluntary torque = measured torque − passive torque. This is the target ("answer key").
  • Passive torque comes from the passive trials: the person relaxes at each position before the pushing trial. One value per position and session (supervisor’s rule). For YES the values are the experimenter’s worksheet readings of the same passive holds.
  • We score each session with its own passive trials.
  • Ankle position in radians. Negative = toes down (plantarflexion), positive = toes up. The "toes-down positions" are the 2 most negative ones of each person.
  • IES has no passive trials, so for IES nothing is subtracted (raw measured torque).

Why the toes-down positions are hard

  • At the toes-down positions the calf is short and the pushes are small: the voluntary torque there varies by only 0.4–0.8 Nm (standard deviation) for some people, against up to 28 Nm at the strongest position.
  • So an error of 0.5 Nm is invisible at a strong position but ruins the score at a toes-down one.
  • The largest |voluntary torque| per position ranges from about 2.5 Nm (JM, toes-down) to 66 Nm (CH, toes-up). Result slides print it as "peak" so the MAE can be judged against it. Where the relaxed level is not 0 the peak differs from the push: larger at YES −0.49 rad (rest ≈ −2 Nm), smaller at IES’s toes-down positions (rest ≈ +2 Nm, nothing subtracted).

A · Setup

The model (unchanged from last semester)

What it is

  • An LSTM is a network with a small memory: it reads the 20 samples in order and keeps what matters. It then outputs one number, the torque right now.
  • It slides along the trial one sample at a time, so every 10 ms gives a new prediction.
  • 1,001 adjustable numbers in total. One person’s training session gives about 71,000 windows, 57,000 of them used for fitting (they overlap heavily, so they are far fewer independent samples).
  • One model per person, trained on one session and tested on the other one.

Where it comes from

  • Same layout and training settings as Truong et al. (2023, Sensors 23:3331): 2 × LSTM(8), dropout 0.3, Nadam 0.003, batch 8. They used it for EMG → knee and ankle moments while moving.
  • Those settings were not tuned for our task, which is why we tested dropout, learning rate and window length (section C).
  • Window length = 0.2 s (20 samples at 100 Hz) for every model in this deck unless a slide says otherwise.

How we test: the new method

1 · Both directions

  • Learn session 1 → test session 2: the original protocol, our main result.
  • Learn session 2 → test session 1: the same with the sessions swapped. If a change only works one way, it is probably luck.

2 · Repeated training runs (random seeds)

  • Training has randomness: the starting values of the 1,001 numbers, the order of the windows, and the dropout pattern. A "seed" fixes that randomness.
  • We train the same model on the same data with different seeds: 10 runs each for last semester’s model and ours (learn 1 → test 2; 3 in the reverse direction), 3 runs for each of the three changes alone, 2 for the other screened changes (3 for the learning rate).
  • The spread we show comes from these training runs. The test trials are always the same 8 trials per person.

3 · One change at a time, rules written first

  • Each idea changes exactly one thing compared with last semester’s model, everything else stays fixed.
  • The pass/fail rules were written before any result (e.g. toes-down R² up by at least 0.10, no other position worse by more than 0.05). No single change met all of them, mainly because of the run-to-run noise at YES −0.49 rad. We kept the final changes by judgment: they gave the lowest toes-down error and consistent gains at EG −0.74, EG −0.60 and YES −0.38 rad.

4 · Screening, then checking on new people

  • Ideas were first screened on YES and EG, the two lowest toes-down scores in our first 3-run check of last semester’s model (mean R² with each run and position floored at −1: YES 0.05, EG 0.25, JM 0.39; recomputed with today’s answer key).
  • The final combination was then run on all 6 people. IES and HM were not used for any decision in this study, so their results are the cleanest check (report 1 had looked at them with a similar pipeline).

How we score

Per position, per training run

  • R² = 1 − (squared error of the prediction) ÷ (squared spread of the measured torque around its own average). 1 = perfect; 0 = no better than predicting the trial’s average all the time.
  • R² can go below 0 when the prediction is worse than that average, e.g. when the whole curve is shifted up or down. We show any value below 0 as 0 (supervisor’s request); the raw values are kept in the files.
  • MAE (mean absolute error, Nm) = the average size of the error. It replaces RMSE (supervisor’s request).
  • Next to the MAE we give the peak: the largest |voluntary torque| in that test trial, so 0.3 Nm can be judged against a 3 Nm or a 60 Nm push.

Over training runs

  • We report the median, not the mean (one bad run cannot drag it).
  • Spread: 5th–95th percentile of the runs (box plots, tables) and 1st–99th (appendix for our model, dashboard), not the standard deviation.
  • Note: with 10 runs the 1st/99th percentiles are almost the worst/best run.
  • Every person has 8 positions → 8 R² values. Summaries over several positions take the median over runs at each position first, then the median across positions; we never pool all values into one R², because big positions would hide the small ones.

B · Last semester

Why it failed at the toes-down positions: 4 causes we found

1 · A wrong value in the passive table (JM)

  • The automatic table gave JM +11.7 Nm at −0.07 rad (neighbours −0.5 and −3.4 Nm). The old rule accepted two pieces of passive trials in which JM was not relaxed (foot still, but torque varying with an SD of 15–18 Nm).
  • So the training target there was about 13 Nm off.

2 · The EMG size changed between sessions

  • For about the same torque, JM’s calf EMG in session 2 was only about 0.4× its session-1 size (0.36× at −0.64 rad, 0.43× at −0.36 rad); YES’s was about 1.3× at −0.38 rad (earlier analysis, report 1).
  • A network that learned "this much EMG = this much torque" then predicts pushes that are too small or too big.

3 · Training ignored the small positions

  • The loss adds up squared errors in Nm². Big positions have big errors, so they decide the fit.
  • For YES, the 2 toes-down positions hold only about 1 % of the torque variance the loss has to explain, against about 31 % for the strongest position. So plain squared error barely rewards fitting them.

4 · A level shift the EMG cannot see (YES −0.49 rad)

  • At YES −0.49 rad, after subtracting each session’s passive value, the resting level still moved by about 0.7 Nm between sessions, while the pushes there vary by only 0.4 Nm (SD).
  • Most of last semester’s error at the toes-down positions was such an offset (whole curve shifted). The shape of the predictions followed the measured torque well (correlation 0.80–0.99).

C · Change 1

Change 1 — approach: a clean passive table

What we did, step by step

  1. Take the passive trial at one position (person relaxed).
  2. Find the part where the foot is still and the torque is steady: angle wobble below 0.005 rad, torque spread below 1 Nm, for at least 5 s. In practice that is the start of the hold.
  3. Average the torque there. That is the passive value for this position and session.
  4. Repeat for every position. If a position has no clean hold (JM and HM near −0.08 rad), use the straight line between its two neighbours.
  5. Training target = measured − passive value of the training session; answer key for scoring = measured − passive value of the tested session.

Why

  • Removes JM’s wrong entries (+11.7 Nm in session 1, +10.8 Nm in session 2): the clean value there is about −1.9 Nm.
  • For the other people the clean table is identical to the old one (IES has none), so this change only affects JM.
  • Passive torque falls while a joint is held stretched and recovers after release (Duong et al. 2001), and it is lower after strong contractions (Kay & Blazevich 2009). Using the same still part of every hold keeps it consistent.

C · Change 2

Change 2 — approach: EMG auto-gain (automatic volume control per muscle)

Like the automatic volume control of a phone call: if the signal is weaker today, it is turned up, so the network sees the same scale as in training.

What we did

  • Every 0.5 s, for each of the 4 muscles: floor = 10th percentile of its EMG over the past 20 s (its quiet level), top = 95th percentile over the past 45 s (its strong-push level).
  • Scaled EMG = (EMG − floor) ÷ (top − floor), kept between 0 and 2. The range is never allowed below 0.35× the training range, so pure noise is not blown up.
  • At the start of a trial it starts from the training session’s values. It only looks backwards in time and never at torque.
  • It switches itself off for a person whose training pushes go both ways (above +2 Nm and below −2 Nm), because then "quiet" and "strong" are ambiguous. Only CH triggers this.
  • Why: the same push produced a different EMG size in session 2 (cause 2). Running normalisation has been used for EMG movement classification, where it helped when training and test data came from different people (Tanaka et al. 2022, paper slide). It had not been tested across sessions or for torque, which is what we test here.

C · Change 3

Change 3 — approach: equal weight per position

What we did

  • For each training position compute σ = the standard deviation of the voluntary torque there (in the training data). It measures how big the pushes are at that position, not ankle movement.
  • Before training, divide every target by its position’s σ (σ at least 0.1 Nm). After predicting, multiply back by σ, so the output is in Nm again.
  • At a test position the σ of the nearest training position is used. The position is an input, so this needs no test data.
  • This is the same as weighted least squares with weight 1/σ² per position.

C · Window length

Window length: 0.2 s vs 0.4 s vs 0.5 s

Approach

  • The window is how much EMG history the network sees for one prediction: 0.2 s = 20 samples (last semester and ours), 0.4 s = 40, 0.5 s = 50.
  • We tested 0.4 s on last semester’s model (screening, YES + EG) and, after a teammate found 0.5 s lowered his average error, 0.5 s on our final model for all 6 people (one change: the window only).
  • Earlier exploration also had a 0.4 s version of a passive-trial pipeline with a bigger network (16 cells × 2 with per-position experts; 3 people, faster training settings).

Results

  • 0.4 s on last semester’s model (YES + EG, 2 runs): toes-down MAE +24 % (screening slide).
  • 0.4 s in the earlier exploration (bigger network): toes-down R² 0.88 → 0.90, other-position MAE 0.69 → 0.51 Nm (-26 %).
  • 0.5 s on our final model: a small gain at both the toes-down and the other positions, R² about unchanged. Details on the next slide.

E · Concepts

Bias, variance and overfitting in plain words

Bias

The error that remains even with plenty of training: the model is too simple, or the inputs do not contain the information.

How we check: the error on the data the model was fitted on. If even that is high, it is bias.

Variance

How much the result changes when training changes: another random seed, or other training data.

How we check: the spread over repeated training runs (10 seeds), and the gap between the fitting part and the unseen check part of the same session.

Overfitting

The model memorises the training samples, noise included, instead of the general rule. Training error keeps falling while the error on unseen data rises.

How we check: learning curves (error per epoch on fit / check / test), early stopping, dropout and model-size tests.

One more thing that is none of these: a change between sessions

Session 2 is recorded later in the same visit (electrodes left on), after many pushes. For the same push the EMG size changed (JM about 0.4×), and the relaxed torque moved (YES −0.49 rad: about 0.7 Nm). Possible causes are skin–electrode contact, fatigue or muscle history. A model can be perfect on session 1 and still be off on session 2. This is called dataset shift. Regularising the network cannot fix it; auto-gain reduced it but did not remove it. (The clean passive table is a separate fix of a wrong label.)

F · Torque demeaning

Torque demeaning: what it means and what it shows

Approach

  • Demeaned = subtract each trial’s own average from both the measured and the predicted torque before scoring. What is left is the shape: when the pushes happen and how big they are relative to each other.
  • Demeaned R² high, normal R² low → the shape is right but the whole curve is shifted (offset).
  • For scoring this is easy and we report it here.
  • As a training target it is possible, but the model then only predicts changes. To give real torque, a device needs the level from somewhere, e.g. re-zeroing during a known relaxed moment, like taring a scale. Using the test trial’s own average would use the answer, so it cannot be used for the main result.
  • Toes-down R² (all 6 people): last semester 0.77 → 0.91 after demeaning; ours 0.94 → 0.94.
  • The bigger the jump from the coloured to the grey bar, the more of the error is a level shift. The mean of the per-person jumps is +0.19 for last semester and +0.05 for ours; for ours about 69 % of it is YES (+0.19, mainly −0.49 rad); every other person ≤ 0.06.

G · Paper 1

Paper 1 — Schulte et al. (2022): trained EMG models degrade on a new day

Schulte RV, Zondag M, Buurke JH, Prinsen EC (2022). Multi-day EMG-based knee joint torque estimation using hybrid neuromusculoskeletal modelling and convolutional neural networks. Frontiers in Robotics and AI 9:869476. doi:10.3389/frobt.2022.869476

What they did

  • 10 able-bodied people, knee and ankle movements on days 1, 2, 3 and 7.
  • EMG normalised by a maximum push measured again every session.
  • Models trained on day 1 only, tested on held-out day-1 data and on the later days.

What they found

  • Neural network (convolution + LSTM): knee-torque error (NRMSE) 5.8 % on day 1 → 10–11 % on later days.
  • Hybrid CNN + musculoskeletal model: 10.0 % → 12.6–14.1 %. The pure musculoskeletal model (13.4–15.2 %) did not change between days, but was less accurate throughout.
  • Days 2, 3 and 7 did not differ from each other: the jump happens once, when the session changes.

Why it matters for us

  • Same pattern as our check-vs-test gap: a model that looks fine within a session degrades on another session.
  • Supports testing across sessions (as we do): even with the maximum push re-measured every session, accuracy dropped on a new day. It motivates, but does not test, adaptive input scaling such as our auto-gain.
  • Limits: knee, moving legs, a different model; different days, while our sessions are in the same visit with the electrodes left on.

G · Paper 2

Paper 2 — Tanaka et al. (2022): running normalisation of EMG

Tanaka T, Nambu I, Maruyama Y, Wada Y (2022). Sliding-window normalization to improve the performance of machine-learning models for real-time motion prediction using electromyography. Sensors 22(13):5005. doi:10.3390/s22135005

What they did

  • Each EMG sample is rescaled with the mean and spread of its own most recent window (100–500 ms), using only past data and no calibration step.
  • 10 people, 12 elbow-movement conditions, classifier for rest / flexion / extension.

What they found

  • Own-person model: 77.7 % accuracy with sliding-window normalisation, 56.2 % with none, 77.2 % with a fixed scale from training (sliding and fixed were equal).
  • Model trained on 9 other people: 63.1 % vs 41.4 % (none) and 54.4 % (fixed).
  • So it beat a fixed scale only when training and test data came from different people.

Why it matters for us

  • The idea behind our auto-gain: rescale each muscle from its own recent past.
  • We use longer windows (20–45 s) and percentiles (quiet and strong levels), because our pushes last several seconds and a 0.5 s window would erase them.
  • Limits: classification, not torque; elbow; no test across sessions or days, which is what we test.

G · Limitations

Limitations (what a careful reader should know)

  • Rules fixed in advance were not met. The noise and no-regression rules written before the study failed at several stages, and the final combination was chosen by judgment. The evidence for it is the result on people not used for choosing and the consistent gains, not the rules.
  • Not a fully independent test. The components were chosen by looking at session-2 results of some people (YES, EG; JM and CH influenced the auto-gain design). Only IES and HM were not used for any decision in this study (report 1 had already looked at them with a similar pipeline). On them alone: toes-down R² 0.78 → 0.94, MAE 0.92 → 0.41 Nm.
  • JM −0.08 rad is a label fix: it improves because the training target was corrected (change 1), not because the network is better.
  • YES −0.49 rad is worse with our model. Learning on session 1, the resting level moved 0.72 Nm against 0.40 Nm of push variation, a shift the EMG cannot show. Learning on session 2, R² drops from 0.40 to -0.39 with no offset; a likely cause is that auto-gain amplifies noise in a calf that is barely active there.
  • IES has no passive trials; measured torque is used as it is.
  • Offline processing: the EMG smoothing filter looks about 0.25 s into the future (zero-phase), for every model, so comparisons between models stay fair. A causal (real-time) filter adds a delay of about 0.2 s, as long as the input window, so the cost could be noticeable; it has not been measured.
  • Small study: 6 people, 2 sessions in the same visit with the electrodes left on (easier than a different day). Runs: 10 runs each for last semester’s model and ours (learn 1 → test 2); 3 runs in the reverse direction and for each single change; 2 runs in the screening (3 for the learning rate); 3 for the 0.5 s window; 1 for the learning curves.

Conclusions and next steps

Conclusions

  • Changing the inputs and targets (clean passive table, auto-gain) and weighting each position equally improved the toes-down R² from 0.77 to 0.94 and MAE from 0.83 to 0.44 Nm.
