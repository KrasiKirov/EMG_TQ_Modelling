# Verified references for the EXPERIMENTS study

Found by literature agents and independently re-checked by a second agent (title, authors, year, venue, DOI, and that the stated finding is in the paper).
- **comparable** = an EMG/MMG-to-torque regression study.
- **related technique** = the same method, used on a different problem.

The literature motivates experiments; it does not decide them. Our own runs decide.

## [X01] Time course of stress relaxation and recovery in human ankles
- **Citation:** Duong B, Low M, Moseley AM, Lee RYW, Herbert RD (2001). *Clinical Biomechanics 16(7):601-607*. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0268-0033(01)00043-2
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: measuring passive ankle torque on a dynamometer. It is not an EMG-to-torque regression study.
- **Relevant finding:** In experiment 1 (n=8), relaxed ankles were held at a fixed dorsiflexion angle for 20 min, released for 2 min, then stretched again. In experiment 2 (n=24), a 20-min stretch was followed by a release of 0 to 20 min and a second stretch. Torque was recorded continuously. During a constant-angle stretch, passive torque fell bi-exponentially towards a fitted asymptote of 58% of its starting value, so the largest possible drop was about 42%. Getting half of that drop took nearly 5 min. Within 2 min of release, torque had recovered by 43% (the abstract's figure). Recovery did not appear to differ between subjects who stayed relaxed during the release and those who did isometric contractions. Recovery followed a time course similar to the relaxation.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X01: Passive torque at a fixed angle is not one fixed number. It depends on how long the joint has been held at that angle and on what happened in the previous few minutes. A passive value from a separate passive trial recorded earlier may therefore differ from the passive torque actually present during the active trial (our inference). This supports building the cleaned passive table from a consistent time window, such as the same point in every hold or the rest period just before each contraction.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not give the size of stress relaxation at strongly plantarflexed angles. There the passive torque is small and comes mainly from different structures, the stretched dorsiflexors and front-of-ankle tissues rather than the calf (general knowledge, not from this paper). The 58% asymptote comes from a 20-min hold in the dorsiflexed range, so it does not tell us how big the drop is in the short holds used for a passive table. It does not show how much error this causes in our voluntary-torque targets or in the LSTM's R2.

## [X01] Isometric contractions reduce plantar flexor moment, Achilles tendon stiffness, and neuromuscular activity but remove the subsequent effects of stretch
- **Citation:** Kay AD, Blazevich AJ (2009). *Journal of Applied Physiology 107(4):1181-1189*. https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00281.2009
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: how passive ankle moment changes after contractions on a dynamometer. It is not an EMG-to-torque regression study.
- **Relevant finding:** 16 healthy adults were tested on an isokinetic dynamometer before and after six 8-s maximal isometric plantar flexor contractions. After the contractions, passive ankle joint moment was 4.9% lower and Achilles tendon stiffness 10.9% lower (both P<0.01). Isometric maximal moment did not fall. Peak concentric moment fell 11.5%, and peak triceps surae EMG amplitude fell 21.0%, the two drops being correlated (r=0.90). Three 60-s static stretches done next caused no further significant change. Measured 30 min after those stretches, which was more than 30 min after the contractions, passive moment and tendon stiffness were still significantly reduced.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X01: Passive moment depends on contraction history. Passive trials recorded before the active block, before any strong contractions, may overestimate the passive torque present once the subject has done MVCs (our inference). The reduction was still there more than 30 min later, so it could last through much of a recording session. This supports either re-measuring passive torque after the warm-up and MVC contractions or taking it from rest periods between contractions. It also explains why a table recorded first could be biased in one direction.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** The 4.9% is an average change after maximal contractions. The abstract does not say at which angle passive moment was measured, but it was probably in the stretched, dorsiflexed range. The paper does not give the change in Nm at strongly plantarflexed angles, where passive torque is small. It does not test submaximal tracking contractions, and Duong et al. 2001 found that isometric contractions during a release did not clearly change passive-torque recovery. The 30-min result comes after contractions followed by stretching, so it is not a clean time course of the contraction effect alone. It does not say whether this bias is big enough to explain the R2 drop in our model.

## [X01] A new method for gravity correction of dynamometer data and determining passive elastic moments at the joint
- **Citation:** Anderson DE, Nussbaum MA, Madigan ML (2010). *Journal of Biomechanics 43(6):1220-1223*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbiomech.2009.11.036
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: separating gravity and passive torque from dynamometer data. It is not an EMG-to-torque regression study.
- **Relevant finding:** Dynamometer torque contains muscle torque plus a gravity moment and a passive elastic moment, and both must be estimated and subtracted to isolate the muscle torque. The authors modelled passive torque as a general sinusoid for gravity (A1 sin θ + A2 cos θ) plus two exponential terms for passive tissue, one for each end of the range. They fitted it in steps to slow (5 deg/s) passive sweeps through the full range, two cycles per joint, from 20 healthy older adults aged 55-73 on a Biodex System 3. The gravity term was fitted only within a middle 'gravity-only region' (40% of the range, where passive elastic torque was assumed negligible). The exponentials were then fitted to what was left at each end. Mean concordance with measured passive torque was 0.981 at the ankle. Within the gravity-only region, the ankle RMS error was 0.641 Nm with the new method versus 0.972 Nm with the usual single-point sine method (p<0.001).
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X01: Rather than a lookup table with one passive value per angle, where a single bad entry corrupts that angle, fit a smooth gravity-plus-exponential curve across the angles for each subject and session. A bad entry then shows up as a clear outlier from the curve and can be replaced by the fitted value. The ankle residuals here (about 0.6-1.0 Nm) are of similar size to the 0.4-0.8 Nm spread of voluntary torque at our plantarflexed angles (our inference). So small passive or gravity errors could swamp the signal at those angles, but not at strong angles where the spread is about 15 Nm.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** The subjects were older adults, and the data were dense, continuous slow sweeps rather than static holds at a few fixed angles. Fitting this 6-parameter model to only 8 static points per subject and session is untested and may overfit. Hysteresis was reduced by fitting both sweep directions together, so stress relaxation during a hold is not modelled. The RMS errors are fit residuals in the middle 'gravity-only' region, the same region used for fitting. They are not checked against an independent ground truth and say nothing about the end ranges where the passive exponentials dominate. The gravity-only-region assumption was challenged in a published letter (Clarke, Martin, Herbert 2010, J Biomech 43(13):2653-2655, with an author reply). The paper does not test effects on active torque estimates or on any EMG model.

## [X01] Mechanomyogram amplitude vs. isometric ankle plantarflexion torque of human medial gastrocnemius muscle at different ankle joint angles
- **Citation:** Shi F, Rymer WZ, Son J (2021). *Journal of Electromyography and Kinesiology 61:102609*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jelekin.2021.102609
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: muscle-signal amplitude (MMG) versus isometric plantarflexion torque at several ankle angles, including a strongly plantarflexed one, in healthy adults. EMG was recorded but used only for a load-sharing ratio. There is no EMG-to-torque model.
- **Relevant finding:** 10 healthy adults did isometric plantarflexion at three ankle angles: 26 deg plantarflexion, 10 deg plantarflexion and 3 deg dorsiflexion. At each angle they did one passive trial, three MVCs and three holds at each of 10, 20, 30, 50 and 70% MVC, with 30-s breaks. Each hold started from rest and was then held at the target for at least 6 s. EMG (MG, LG, SOL, TA), MMG and torque were recorded. Passive torque was estimated as the mean of the first 1 s of filtered torque, averaged over all trials at an angle, so one value per angle per subject rather than one per trial. It was then expressed relative to the 26-deg plantarflexed angle. Passive torque increased significantly as the ankle was dorsiflexed (F(2,27)=25.85, p<0.0001), as did maximum torque (F(2,27)=17.98, p<0.0001). The contraction torque was the mean of a 4-s steady segment. The paper does not describe subtracting passive torque from it.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X01: A recent example of taking the passive estimate from the resting start of the contraction trials themselves, at the same angle, rather than relying only on a separate passive trial. That estimate is closer in time and in contraction history to the active data, which addresses the stress-relaxation and recording-order concerns. In X01 the cleaned table could be checked against, or replaced by, pre-contraction rest baselines from our own active trials. Note that Shi et al. averaged these baselines across trials and used them only to describe passive torque, not to correct the voluntary torque.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not compare passive-torque estimation methods, and it does not show that a rest-baseline estimate is more accurate than a separate passive table. It does not use the passive estimate to correct the voluntary torque. Because the most plantarflexed angle was the zero reference, there is no absolute passive torque at the angle where our model fails. A rest baseline can also include small residual muscle activity before the contraction. It does not address EMG-to-torque model accuracy or session-to-session transfer.

## [X02] A Theoretically Grounded Application of Dropout in Recurrent Neural Networks
- **Citation:** Yarin Gal, Zoubin Ghahramani (2016). *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 29 (NIPS/NeurIPS 2016)*. https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2016/hash/076a0c97d09cf1a0ec3e19c7f2529f2b-Abstract.html (arXiv:1512.05287)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (dropout placement and rate in LSTM/GRU, tested on language modelling and sentiment classification, not EMG regression)
- **Relevant finding:** Earlier work had concluded that dropout should only be used on the inputs and outputs of an RNN, because noise in the recurrent connections was thought to drown the signal. The authors derived a 'variational' dropout for LSTM and GRU networks. It samples one dropout mask per sequence and reuses it at every time step, on the inputs, the outputs and the recurrent connections. The older 'naive' approach samples a new mask at each time step and does not drop the recurrent connections. On Penn Treebank language modelling the large variational LSTM reached 73.4 test perplexity, the best single-model result at the time. That figure used MC dropout (1,000 test-time passes) and untied weights; with the standard test-time approximation it was about 75.0, against 78.4 for the earlier naive-dropout model. The best dropout rates for their method were lower than for naive dropout, even though more of the network was dropped. For the large model they were 0.3 (embeddings and recurrent connections) and 0.5 (inputs and outputs), against 0.6. For the medium model they were 0.2 and 0.35, against 0.5. On a small sentiment-analysis dataset (5,000 film reviews), the variational LSTM was the only LSTM that did not overfit. An appendix experiment found that weight decay still matters when this kind of dropout is used.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X02 sweeps the dropout rate (0, 0.1 and 0.5, against the current 0.3). This paper is the standard reference for where dropout belongs in an LSTM, and its own grid search shows the best rate depends on the model and task. In our code (ML/models/lstm.py), dropout is a separate Keras Dropout(0.3) layer after each LSTM layer. After the first layer (return_sequences=True) this draws a new mask at each time step, which is the 'naive' style, and it never touches the recurrent connections. So X02 tests only the rate of naive between-layer dropout. An optional follow-up after X02 is Keras LSTM(dropout=..., recurrent_dropout=...), which reuses one mask across time steps (the variational style). That would be a separate single change.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not give a good dropout rate for our 1,001-parameter, 8-unit regression LSTM with 5 inputs. It does not say whether dropout helps or hurts when the target varies only about 0.4-0.8 Nm (SD), as at our plantarflexed positions. Its models were far larger (650-1,500 units per layer), used word embeddings, and did classification and language tasks. The finding that variational dropout beats naive dropout was not tested on biosignal regression.

## [X02] BiLSTM-Based Joint Torque Prediction From Mechanomyogram During Isometric Contractions: A Proof of Concept Study
- **Citation:** Jongsang Son, Fandi Shi, William Zev Rymer (2024). *IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering, vol. 32, pp. 1926-1933*. https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3399121
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** comparable (isometric ankle plantarflexion torque regression with an LSTM; but the input is MMG, not surface EMG)
- **Relevant finding:** The model predicted average isometric ankle plantarflexion torque (normalised to body mass, Nm/kg) from mechanomyogram (MMG) signals. The MMG came from a 3-axis accelerometer on the medial gastrocnemius. The networks were stacked bidirectional LSTMs, evaluated by leave-one-subject-out validation on 10 subjects, with each validation repeated 10 times. The authors crossed 4 architectures, 5 unit counts (32-512) and 4 dropout ratios (0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8). Dropout ratio had a significant effect on every metric (p<0.0001), but which rate was 'best' depended on the metric. Dropout 0.8 gave significantly smaller errors, but the differences were tiny (MAE 0.216 vs 0.219 Nm/kg for dropout 0.0). It also gave significantly worse regression slope and R2 (0.668 vs 0.701 for dropout 0.0). The paper is inconsistent about the dropout-0.8 slope: the text says 0.771, while Table IV says 0.701. Dropout 0.0 gave the best slope (0.857; 0.0, 0.2 and 0.5 did not differ significantly from each other) and the best R2 (0.701, significantly higher than all other rates). The interaction between unit count and dropout ratio was significant for all metrics, so the best dropout depended on network size. The final chosen model used 2 BiLSTM layers, 512 units and no dropout.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** This is a close published example of a dropout sweep for LSTM torque prediction in an isometric ankle task, and it supports including 0 in X02. It also shows that heavy dropout can slightly lower average error while lowering the slope of predicted vs measured torque, so the predicted range is compressed. If that happened in our model, it could hurt the low-variance plantarflexed positions most; this is our inference, not a result of the paper. Our X02 report should therefore show per-position R2 and the predicted-vs-measured slope, not only overall MSE, because the 'best' dropout can change with the metric. The dropout-by-size interaction is a further reason not to borrow a rate tuned on large networks for our 8-unit model.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** The signals were MMG from one muscle, not 4-channel surface EMG. The model predicted one average torque per trial (many-to-one), not a continuous torque time series. Only one ankle angle was used (10 degrees plantarflexion), so the paper says nothing about the extreme plantarflexed positions. The networks were much larger (32-512 units, bidirectional, trained with Adam). Validation was across subjects, not across sessions within one subject. The error differences between dropout rates were very small, so the paper does not show that dropout meaningfully changes accuracy.

## [X02 / X03] EMG-Based Estimation of Lower Limb Joint Angles and Moments Using Long Short-Term Memory Network
- **Citation:** Minh Tat Nhat Truong, Amged Elsheikh Abdelgadir Ali, Dai Owaki, Mitsuhiro Hayashibe (2023). *Sensors (MDPI), vol. 23, no. 6, article 3331*. https://doi.org/10.3390/s23063331 (open access, PMC10058035)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** comparable (sEMG to joint moment regression with a small stacked LSTM, including ankle moment)
- **Relevant finding:** The model predicted knee and ankle angles and moments (8 outputs: both sides) from 112 sEMG features (14 muscles x 8 features) in 6 healthy males. Each subject had a personalised model. It used two stacked LSTM layers of 8 hidden units each, 30% dropout, a 20-step (1 s) input window, MSE loss and batch size 8. Training used the Nadam optimiser 'with a reduced learning rate starting from 0.003 and a reduced factor of 70%' (the paper's wording; no patience is given). The authors say there is no specific rule for choosing hyperparameters and that they chose them by repeatedly adjusting and retraining. They also kept the parameter count small to avoid overfitting. Ankle moment was the hardest output: mean R2 85.44% +/- 8.14%, and only 4 of 12 ankle-moment outputs exceeded 90% R2.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** Our baseline is essentially this configuration: 2 x LSTM(8), dropout 0.3, Nadam at 0.003, LR_FACTOR 0.7, batch 8 and a 20-step window (ML/models/lstm.py, ML/config.py). These values were found by trial and error for a different task. The paper reports no comparison of settings and no repeated seeds. So for our isometric, 5-input, cross-session problem they are inherited defaults, not tuned values. That is the direct reason for X02 (dropout) and X03 (learning rate). The paper's weakest output was also the ankle moment, which is consistent with ankle torque being harder to predict from EMG.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not show that dropout 0.3 or learning rate 0.003 is optimal, even for their own data, because no comparison is reported. Their task was dynamic (squatting, picking up an object, and sit-to-stand; the authors say walking was not covered). It used 112 EMG features and a chronological 60/20/20 split of one continuous ~5-minute recording. So it says nothing about isometric holds, low-torque plantarflexed positions, or between-session EMG changes.

## [X03] Incorporating Nesterov Momentum into Adam
- **Citation:** Timothy Dozat (2016). *ICLR 2016 Workshop Track (earlier version: Stanford CS229 project report, 2015)*. https://openreview.net/forum?id=OM0jvwB8jIp57ZJjtNEZ (CS229 version: https://cs229.stanford.edu/proj2015/054_report.pdf)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (optimiser design; benchmarks were word embeddings, image classification and LSTM language modelling)
- **Relevant finding:** This is the paper that introduced Nadam: Adam with its classical momentum replaced by Nesterov-style momentum. The author argues that Nesterov momentum gives a better update direction. In the CS229 version, nine optimisers were compared on three benchmarks: word2vec, an MNIST CNN, and a 2-layer, 200-cell LSTM language model. Only the learning rate differed between algorithms. Nadam beat Adam on word2vec (test loss 0.284 vs 0.325) and on the LSTM (test perplexity 105.5 vs 111.0). On the MNIST test set Adam was slightly better (0.0175 vs 0.0183). On the LSTM, plain gradient descent and classical momentum beat all adaptive methods (about 99-101 perplexity), and the author notes that tuning hyperparameters made little difference there. The author concluded that Nadam's improvement over Adam was large in most cases, but that Nadam was not always the best choice.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X03 keeps Nadam and changes only the learning rate (0.001 vs 0.003). This is the primary citation for the optimiser we use. It supports Nadam as a reasonable choice over Adam, but it does not show Nadam is the best optimiser for LSTMs, and it gives no guidance on the learning rate. That supports leaving the optimiser fixed and testing the learning rate as a single change.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not say which learning rate suits Nadam on our model, or whether 0.001 or 0.003 is better for a tiny EMG regression LSTM. The experiments were small and preliminary, single-run, with no run-to-run spread. It is a workshop paper, which had lighter review than a full conference paper.

## [X03] LSTM: A Search Space Odyssey
- **Citation:** Klaus Greff, Rupesh K. Srivastava, Jan Koutnik, Bas R. Steunebrink, Juergen Schmidhuber (2017). *IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, vol. 28, no. 10, pp. 2222-2232*. https://doi.org/10.1109/TNNLS.2016.2582924 (arXiv:1503.04069)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (LSTM hyperparameter importance on speech, handwriting and music sequence tasks)
- **Relevant finding:** The study ran 5,400 random-search training runs: 27 searches of 200 trials, covering the standard LSTM plus 8 variants on speech (TIMIT), handwriting (IAM Online) and music (JSB Chorales) tasks. It used fANOVA to split test-performance variance across hyperparameters. The learning rate was by far the most important hyperparameter, always explaining more than two-thirds of the variance. Hidden size came second, then input noise, and momentum explained under 1%. Each dataset had a wide basin of good learning rates (up to two orders of magnitude) with little change in performance inside it. The sweet spot was at the high end of the basin, though for 2 of 3 datasets it lay outside the searched range. Interactions between hyperparameters explained 5-20% of the variance, more for TIMIT. From the small learning-rate x hidden-size interaction, the authors suggest a learning rate can be tuned on a small network and then reused on a large one. They did not test this transfer directly.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** This is the main evidence that, for LSTMs, the learning rate matters more than other hyperparameters, which justifies X03 as its own experiment. It also sets expectations. 0.001 and 0.003 differ by only a factor of 3, so both may sit in the same good basin and the difference could be small. X03 therefore needs repeated seeds (see the SEEDS entries) to tell a real effect from noise. The weak interaction with hidden size also suggests a learning rate chosen in X03 is a reasonable starting point for the Tier 2 '16 units' model, though it should be rechecked there.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** They trained with per-sequence SGD with Nesterov momentum, not Adam or Nadam, so the learning-rate ranges (searched from 1e-6 to 1e-2) do not transfer directly to adaptive optimisers. The tasks were classification and sequence modelling, not regression from biosignals. Their networks had 20-200 units per layer, against our 8. Dropout was not studied (they studied input noise instead).

## [X03] Descending through a Crowded Valley - Benchmarking Deep Learning Optimizers
- **Citation:** Robin M. Schmidt, Frank Schneider, Philipp Hennig (2021). *Proceedings of the 38th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2021), PMLR vol. 139, pp. 9367-9376*. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/schmidt21a.html (arXiv:2007.01547)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (optimiser and learning-rate choice across image, VAE and RNN workloads; no EMG or biomechanics)
- **Relevant finding:** The authors benchmarked 15 popular optimisers, including Adam and Nadam, on 8 deep-learning problems, one of them a character-level RNN. The study used 4 tuning budgets and 4 learning-rate schedules, giving 53,760 unique runs. Optimiser performance varied greatly from task to task, and no optimiser clearly won everywhere. Trying several optimisers at their default settings worked about as well as heavily tuning one. Adam, Nadam and RAdam, among others, had good default settings on several (but not all) problems, and Adam remained a strong contender. Tuning and schedules helped on average but varied widely by problem. For example, medium-budget tuning gave a median relative improvement of about 3.4% over defaults, and large-budget tuning plus a trapezoidal schedule gave about 5.2%. Tuning used a single seed, but the best setting was rerun with 10 seeds because tuning can pick 'lucky' seeds.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** This recent, large benchmark supports the narrow design of X03: keep Nadam and change only the learning rate. Adaptive optimisers like Nadam often work well near their defaults, so a large gain from moving 0.003 to 0.001 should not be expected. Our setup also reduces the learning rate on plateaus (LR_FACTOR 0.7, LR_PATIENCE 10), and the paper found that schedules change outcomes in problem-specific ways. Finally, they reran the winning setting with 10 seeds, which supports running each X03 setting with several seeds before declaring a winner.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It gives no recommended learning rate for our model. Its test problems were much larger and mostly classification or generative models. The schedules tested (constant, cosine, cosine with warm restarts, trapezoidal) did not include reduce-on-plateau, which we use. It does not tell us whether the learning rate affects the low-torque plantarflexed positions differently from the strong positions.

## [X04] Multi-Task Learning Using Uncertainty to Weigh Losses for Scene Geometry and Semantics
- **Citation:** Alex Kendall, Yarin Gal, Roberto Cipolla (2018). *IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR 2018), pp. 7482-7491*. https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR.2018.00781 (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07115)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: per-task inverse-variance loss weighting in multi-task deep learning (computer vision), not EMG.
- **Relevant finding:** Multi-task results depend strongly on how the task losses are weighted, and the best weighting depends on each task's measurement scale and noise level. The authors derive a loss that multiplies each regression task's squared error by 1/(2*sigma^2) and adds a log(sigma) penalty, with sigma (the task's noise level) learned during training. A noisier task gets a smaller weight. On a three-task benchmark (Tiny CityScapes: semantic segmentation, instance segmentation and depth), segmentation IoU was 50.1% with uniform weights, 62.8% with approximately optimal grid-searched weights and 63.4% with learned uncertainty weights. The single-task segmentation model scored 59.4%. Depth error fell from 0.640 (single task) to 0.522. A two-task comparison showed the learned weights doing slightly better than weights from a fine-grained grid search. An appendix shows the learned weights are robust to how sigma is initialised.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X04 treats each of the 8 ankle angles as a separate task. Right now the plain Nm^2 MSE is dominated by the strong angles (target std about 15 Nm, against 0.4-0.8 Nm at the plantarflexed angles). Kendall et al. are the standard reference for scaling each task's squared error by an inverse variance. They also suggest a follow-up to fixed 1/std weights: learn one log-sigma per angle.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** Their weight is the inverse of each task's residual noise variance, not of the target's spread. Dividing our targets by each angle's std equalises scale, which is a different quantity. If the plantarflexed targets are mostly passive-torque estimation error (X01), up-weighting them will push the model to fit that noise. The paper says nothing about tiny recurrent models, about 6 subjects, or about EMG.

## [X04] Delving into Deep Imbalanced Regression
- **Citation:** Yuzhe Yang, Kaiwen Zha, Yingcong Chen, Hao Wang, Dina Katabi (2021). *Proceedings of the 38th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2021), PMLR 139:11842-11851*. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/yang21m.html (arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09554)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: reweighting by target density in deep imbalanced regression (images, text, health data), not EMG.
- **Relevant finding:** With a continuous target, a plain loss fits the well-represented target ranges and does badly where data are sparse. On IMDB-WIKI-DIR age estimation, few-shot-region MAE was 26.33 with plain training. It fell to 22.76 with square-root inverse-frequency loss weighting (SQINV), to 22.51 with SQINV plus kernel-smoothed label density (LDS), and to 22.19 with SQINV+LDS+FDS. Many-shot MAE was essentially unchanged (7.23 plain, 7.24 SQINV, 7.20 SQINV+LDS+FDS). Full inverse-frequency weighting (INV) helped the few-shot region about as much (22.83) but hurt the many-shot region (MAE 7.64 against 7.23).
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X04 assumes we can re-weight the loss to help the under-served angles without hurting the strong ones. This paper is evidence that loss reweighting can do this in deep regression. Its INV against SQINV result also matters: full inverse weighting cost accuracy in the well-served region, while the square-root version did not. That argues for testing 1/sqrt(std) alongside full 1/std per-angle weights.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** Their imbalance is rare target values. Ours is a group (angle) whose targets have a tiny spread. Small torques are actually common at every angle, so their density-based weights would not single out the plantarflexed angles. Results come from large datasets and big CNNs (ResNet-50 for IMDB-WIKI-DIR), not a ~1,000-parameter LSTM trained on 6 subjects.

## [X04] Density-based weighting for imbalanced regression
- **Citation:** Michael Steininger, Konstantin Kobs, Padraig Davidson, Anna Krause, Andreas Hotho (2021). *Machine Learning, 110(8), 2187-2211*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-021-06023-5 (open copy: https://opus.bibliothek.uni-wuerzburg.de/files/26917/Steininger_Machine.pdf)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Related technique: cost-sensitive (weighted) loss for imbalanced regression with neural networks. Applications include precipitation downscaling, not EMG.
- **Relevant finding:** DenseLoss weights each training sample by how rare its target value is, using kernel density estimation. A single hyperparameter, alpha, sets how strong the weighting is: alpha = 0 gives uniform weights, i.e. ordinary MSE. The weights are rescaled to a mean of 1 so the learning rate does not need retuning. On synthetic data, alpha = 1 cut RMSE in the rarest bin by 1.21-7.02, but raised it in the most common bin by 1.12-1.68. Alpha above 1 hurt common bins badly, and sometimes the rarest bin too. Across 20 benchmark datasets, normalised MAE fell by roughly 10% for rare points while error rose for common points. DenseLoss mostly beat the SMOGN resampling method. As future work, the authors propose ensembles of models trained with different alpha values, each an expert for part of the target range.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** X04 should be run as a dial, not an on/off switch. For example, weight each angle by std^(-alpha) with alpha in {0, 0.5, 1}, rescale the weights to a mean of 1, and report per-angle R2 for each setting so the trade-off between strong and weak angles is visible. Their 'expert per target range' idea also ties X04 to the Tier 2 per-angle-experts plan.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It weights by how rare each target value is, not by angle group or target scale. It gives no evidence that weighting helps when the under-served group's targets are partly measurement noise, which the passive-torque errors may make ours. It shows the opposite of a free lunch: with alpha = 1, accuracy on the common cases got measurably worse, so X04 may cost accuracy at the strong angles.

- **Citation:** Xavier Bouthillier, Pierre Delaunay, Mirko Bronzi, Assya Trofimov, Brennan Nichyporuk, Justin Szeto, Nazanin Mohammadi Sepahvand, Edward Raff, Kanika Madan, Vikram Voleti, Samira Ebrahimi Kahou, Vincent Michalski, Tal Arbel, Chris Pal, Gael Varoquaux, Pascal Vincent (2021). *Proceedings of Machine Learning and Systems 3 (MLSys 2021)*. https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2021/hash/0184b0cd3cfb185989f858a1d9f5c1eb-Abstract.html (arXiv:2103.03098)

- **Type:** related technique (benchmarking methodology; image, NLP and medical-imaging tasks, no EMG)

The 0.75 threshold was calibrated on their 5 tasks, not on a small-subject regression study.

## [SEEDS (also informs X02 and X03)] Reporting Score Distributions Makes a Difference: Performance Study of LSTM-networks for Sequence Tagging
- **Citation:** Nils Reimers, Iryna Gurevych (2017). *Proceedings of the 2017 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP 2017), pp. 338-348*. https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/D17-1035
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (LSTM training variance and hyperparameter study on NLP sequence tagging; classification, not regression)
- **Relevant finding:** The study evaluated about 50,000 LSTM networks. Changing only the random seed moved a published NER system (Lample et al.) between 89.99% and 91.00% F1 over 86 seeds, a statistically significant difference (p<1e-4). They also took 1,830 configurations and trained each twice with different seeds. The median gap was 0.17-0.72 F1 points depending on the task, and the worst case was 8.2 points (82.5% vs 74.3%). The authors recommend comparing score distributions from many runs instead of single scores. In the same study, Nadam gave the best result of six optimisers in 51.4% of 882 configurations and converged fastest (about 9 vs 42 epochs for SGD on NER). Adam, Nadam and RMSProp had about 35% lower spread than Adagrad. Variational dropout (Gal and Ghahramani) beat both naive dropout (a new mask at every time step) and no dropout in 83.5% of 479 configurations, and had the smallest spread. Applying it to both the output and recurrent connections was best in 62.6% of cases. Two stacked LSTM layers worked best (1 and 2 were on par for POS), and the number of recurrent units mattered very little.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** This is the clearest LSTM-specific evidence that the seed alone can produce differences as large as a typical 'improvement'. Each X02 and X03 setting therefore needs several seeds, reported as mean +/- SD or as all individual runs. It also backs our choice of Nadam. Its 'naive dropout' (new mask every time step) matches what our Keras Dropout layer does, so it gives empirical support, beyond Gal 2016, for testing variational or recurrent dropout as a follow-up to X02.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** The seed spread was measured as F1 on text-tagging tasks with much larger BiLSTM(-CRF) models (25-125 units per layer, word embeddings). So the size of the spread does not transfer to our 1,001-parameter torque regressor or to per-position R2. Their dropout rates (0.05-0.5) were tested on much larger networks. Nadam being best there does not mean it is best for EMG data.

## [SEEDS (run-to-run variability; applies to X01-X06)] Nondeterminism and Instability in Neural Network Optimization
- **Citation:** Cecilia Summers, Michael J. Dinneen (2021). *Proceedings of the 38th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2021), PMLR vol. 139, pp. 9913-9922*. https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/summers21a.html
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** related technique (training nondeterminism in image CNNs and a small recurrent language model; no EMG or regression)
- **Relevant finding:** Using 100 runs per condition, the authors varied one source of training randomness at a time: weight initialisation, data shuffling, data augmentation, GPU (cuDNN) nondeterminism, and stochastic operations such as dropout. Every source produced about the same run-to-run spread. For ResNet-14 on CIFAR-10 the test-accuracy SD was about 0.21-0.26 percentage points; for a small recurrent (QRNN) language model the perplexity SD was about 0.18-0.20. Changing a single initial weight by the smallest possible floating-point step gave about as much spread as all sources combined. The authors attribute this to training instability. Linear models did not show it (accuracy SD 0), but it appeared as soon as one hidden layer was added. Accelerated ensembling and test-time augmentation reduced the spread partly, but the authors say they are far from solving the problem.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** Fixing a single seed does not remove run-to-run noise; it only hides it. Any change in X01-X06, even a different dropout rate that changes the random draws, puts the run on a different training path. A 2-layer LSTM is a non-convex network with hidden layers, so by the paper's evidence it is likely in the unstable regime. This is our inference; the paper tested CNNs and a QRNN, not LSTMs. This supports running each arm with several seeds and comparing distributions, especially for the noisy plantarflexed-position R2. Since several seeds are trained anyway, averaging their predictions (a small seed ensemble) can also be reported as a way to reduce variance at no extra training cost.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** It does not measure the spread for our model, data or metrics, so the number of seeds we need must be estimated from our own runs. Its absolute numbers (accuracy SD, perplexity SD) cannot be converted to R2 or Nm. Our runs are single-threaded CPU TensorFlow with fixed seeds, so the cuDNN/GPU part does not apply and a given seed should be repeatable. The instability finding still means that any setting change gives a new, partly random outcome.

## [Context (LSTM EMG->torque); also Tier 2] Estimation of Joint Torque by EMG-Driven Neuromusculoskeletal Models and LSTM Networks
- **Citation:** Zhang L, Soselia D, Wang R, Gutierrez-Farewik EM (2023). *IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering 31:3722-3731*. https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSRE.2023.3315373 (PMID 37708013)
- **Verified:** yes
- **Type:** Comparable: EMG (plus kinematics) to lower-limb joint torque with LSTM networks, including ankle plantar/dorsiflexion torque.
- **Relevant finding:** The authors estimated ankle dorsi/plantarflexion, knee flexion/extension, hip flexion/extension and hip abduction/adduction torques from EMG plus kinematics during 10 daily movements. They used EMG-driven neuromusculoskeletal (NMS) models and LSTM networks, with inverse-dynamics torque as ground truth. Three setups were compared: Pooled (one model for all movements), Individual (one model per movement) and Clustered (one model per group of similar movements). The abstract says the Pooled, Individual and Clustered LSTMs all had relatively high accuracy. For NMS models, the Individual and Clustered versions did similarly well, while the Pooled NMS model 'may be too generic'. Clustering improved NMS accuracy in some movements but made relatively little difference for the LSTMs, which were already accurate.
- **Why it motivates the experiment:** This establishes LSTMs as a standard, competitive tool for estimating joint torque from EMG, including at the ankle. It also gives a direct comparison for Tier 2: for LSTMs, splitting by condition (per-movement or per-cluster models) made little difference compared with one pooled model. So per-angle experts are not guaranteed to help, and the result should be compared against a pooled, angle-conditioned LSTM.
- **What it does NOT establish for our data:** The task was dynamic daily movements with inverse-dynamics torque, not isometric voluntary torque with passive torque removed. The LSTMs also received kinematics, which gives them condition information our model may lack. The abstract gives no numbers and no results for low-variance conditions. The pooled-vs-individual comparison was across movements, not ankle angles. Network size was not checked, so the result should not be assumed to hold for an 8-unit LSTM.
